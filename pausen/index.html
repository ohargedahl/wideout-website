Pausen · iPad & iPhone

A quiet thing to look at.

One soft shape drifts across the screen. It notices a touch and answers gently. Over the minutes you chose, the light fades — and then it stops, with nothing left to press.

This will not teach your child anything, and it never claims to. It is the least-bad screen for the times you have no choice — a car seat, a plane, a waiting room — and it ends by itself.

The hand-back is the hard part

Most apps aimed at small children are built to be hard to put down. That is the whole business model: another level, another reward, another thing that flashes the moment attention drifts. It works, and it is exactly why handing the device over feels like a decision you will pay for later.

Pausen is built around the opposite goal. It has no score, no levels, no unlocks, no adverts and no next thing. It has an ending. The screen dims from the first second to the last, so the finish is somewhere you arrive rather than somewhere you are stopped, and by the time it goes dark there is nothing on screen worth holding on to.

What it actually does

One shape, moving slowly

A single friendly figure drifts across a warm background at a pace closer to a cloud than a cartoon. Calm is not the same as empty: very young children look away from things that are too simple just as readily as from things that are too busy, so there is always something quietly happening — just never anything sudden.

It answers a touch

A tap makes a soft ring spread out from the fingertip, and the figure glances over. A palm flat on the glass does not make five of them — responses are deliberately capped, so hammering the screen never turns into a light show that rewards the hammering.

You choose how long

Three, five, eight, twelve or twenty minutes. The fade is paced to whichever you pick, so a three-minute session and a twenty-minute session both end the same way: dark, still, and finished.

Bedtime dimming

An optional black background with warm, low-blue shapes instead of the bright ones. Much less light in a dark room. It is not a sleep aid and we will not describe it as one.

Sound, if you want it

Off by default. Switched on, each touch plays a soft note from a single gentle scale, over a quiet rushing background. Everything on screen reads perfectly without it, because the main use for this app is a plane.

Works with no signal at all

There is no network code in the app. It behaves identically in airplane mode, in a tunnel, and at 10,000 metres — because there is nothing for it to reach for.

Stopping them from swiping out: Guided Access

"It ends by itself" is worth nothing if a swipe puts a one-year-old into YouTube ten seconds later. No app can prevent that on its own — iOS deliberately reserves that power for the operating system, so that no app can ever trap you inside itself. The feature that does it is called Guided Access, it is built into every iPhone and iPad, it is free, and it takes about a minute to set up once.

Pausen shows you, live on its own first screen, whether Guided Access is currently on — so you never hand over an unlocked iPad because you thought you had done it.

Set it up once

  1. Settings → Accessibility → Guided Access → turn it on.
  2. Tap Passcode Settings, set a Guided Access passcode, and turn on Face ID or Touch ID as well — so you can get out with a glance rather than typing while holding a child.

Every time you hand it over

  1. Open Pausen.
  2. Triple-click the side button on an iPhone, the top button on most iPads, or the Home button where there is one. Pausen names the right one for the device in your hand.
  3. Tap Start. To finish, triple-click again and use Face ID or your passcode.

Nothing happened when you triple-clicked?

This is the one thing that catches almost everybody, and it is not your fault. The triple-click is a shared shortcut. If it was ever bound to something else — Zoom, Colour Filters, Magnifier, a screen tint you set up months ago — then triple-clicking fires that instead, and Guided Access simply never starts, with no explanation.

Fix it at Settings → Accessibility → Accessibility Shortcut, at the very bottom of the page, and tick Guided Access. Leaving several ticked is fine — triple-clicking then shows a short menu and you choose. Pausen cannot read that list, which is why it can only point you at it.

What it blocks, and what it doesn't

While Guided Access is running it blocks the swipe-up, the app switcher, Control Centre and Notification Centre, and suppresses notifications and calls. You can also switch off the hardware buttons for the session, or circle a region of the screen to make it ignore touches.

Two honest caveats: a force-restart or a flat battery ends the session and the device comes back unlocked, and anyone who knows the Guided Access passcode can leave. Don't make it the same as your device passcode if the child has ever watched you type that one.

For a very young child this is genuinely enough — blocking the swipe is the whole problem, and Guided Access blocks it completely.

Nothing is collected, and there is nothing to opt out of

Pausen has no accounts, no sign-in, no adverts, no purchases inside the app, and no analytics. It contains no third-party code of any kind — no advertising networks, no attribution kits, no crash-reporting services. It never makes a network request, so nothing about your child or your device can leave, because there is no route out.

What it remembers is your own settings — the length you picked, and whether bedtime dimming and sound are on — plus a single number that lets a session pick up near where the last one finished. All of it on the device, all of it deleted with the app.
